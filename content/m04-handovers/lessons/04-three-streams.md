## The one idea to keep

The same template works for every handover, but each handover loses different
things. Change the emphasis and the extra questions to match the handover, and
keep a person in the conversation.

## Same template, different emphasis

| Handover | What gets lost most often | Extra prompt to add |
|---|---|---|
| **Connections to compliance** | Commitments made during negotiation, and the *reasons* behind agreed positions | "Why was [position] agreed, and what was traded for it?" |
| **Compliance to service** | Marginal test results, conditions attached to approvals, data the operator will ask for later | "Which hold point results were marginal or conditional? What follow-up did the operator ask for?" |
| **Service to next project (reuse)** | Lessons that would have prevented an issue | "What went wrong on [PROJECT] that we should check for on new projects?" |

## The handover conversation still matters

A pack is not a handover. Book 30 to 45 minutes with the outgoing lead, the
incoming lead and, ideally, the manager. Use the pack as the agenda. The
conversation is where the watch-outs get added and the open questions get
answered.

## The manager's role

You do not write the pack. You:

- **set the standard:** every handover uses the template and is checked,
- **check the commitments section**, because it carries the most risk,
- **protect the time**, because a handover written on the last afternoon fails,
- **close the loop:** a month later, ask the incoming lead "what did the pack
  miss?", and improve the prompt.

## Where this does not work

Copilot's sweep is only as good as the search scope. A handover that depends on
a supplier's phone calls or a site walk will be thin on those points, so the
conversation has to make up for it.

## What would you do?

A colleague is moving from the connections work on one project to the
compliance work on it in December. What would you check personally before you
let the handover meeting go ahead?
